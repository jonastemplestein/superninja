# Teacher talk: how good Nursery and Reception teachers introduce and run a game

26 September 2026. Written by the teacher-talk researcher for the teacher-voice workflow. It is research input for `docs/TEACHER_SCRIPT.md` and the fix plan (docs/FIX_PLAN_PERF_SCRIPT_SOUNDS.md), not the script itself. Every example line below is an illustration of a move. The script designer picks the actual wording, and new lines are recorded the usual way.

It answers Jonas after playtesting the preschool levels with his 3- and 4-year-old (docs/FEEDBACK.md, Round 14):

> "I find that this extremely abbreviated way of talking, it doesn't help at all. Like teachers give, do like way better explanations, right? They say, so first, I'm going to show you how to do it. Are you ready? And this is how this game goes. I will show you this, and you will do that. Do you want to give it a go now? … Or this like weird shouted, listen, I just said, listen. That's super weird in the first dojo level where it just starts with this, and this ear appears. Teachers explain what they're doing."

It has to be read alongside Round 13: the first game was "too long and boring" for the 3-year-old. The two are not in conflict. The fix is not more talk. It is the right talk: a frame, a narrated demonstration and a real handover, said in short, whole, warm sentences, with the child doing something every few seconds.

Sources are numbered in brackets and listed in §6. [L…] are local files, [W…] are web sources fetched for this document, and [P…] are this project's own docs and transcripts.

---

## 0. The short version

1. **Every game starts with a frame.** A teacher says what the game is called, what it's for, and who does what ("First I'll…, then you'll…") before anything happens. Sounds~Write's own guidance says to "State explicitly what the pupils are going to be learning" [L2], and the DfE's Reading Framework asks teachers to "be clear about objectives for any session and make sure that the children understand them" [W1 p50].
2. **"Watch me first" is a demonstration, not a command.** The teacher does one item while saying what she is doing and why. That is the "think aloud" Sounds~Write recommends from Rosenshine [L2][W3]. Today Sensei says "Let me show you!" and then "Tap the sun!", an order, while the paw does it [P6 2:09–2:10].
3. **"Are you ready?" is a real question, and the game waits for the answer.** Children's TV that works waits for the child: *Blue's Clues* pauses "long enough to give the youngest time to think" [W11], and *Play School* opened with "Ready to knock?" [W13]. In Super Ninja the answer has to be a tap.
4. **Never a bare "Listen!".** Teachers signal listening by saying what to listen *for*: "Listen carefully now for how many animals are on the broom" (Development Matters, Reception) [W2]. Sounds~Write's Lesson 1 script opens with "I'm going to say the word 'sat' very slowly. Listen carefully to hear the sounds that make up the word 'sat'." [L1]. The listening walk starts by asking the children "what makes them good at listening" [L3]. An ear icon that simply appears means nothing to a 3-year-old until someone says what it means.
5. **Short, but whole.** About 4–10 words a sentence, one idea each, at most two steps in one instruction for a 3- or 4-year-old [W2]. No telegraphic fragments ("Ninja ears on!"), no chains of commands.
6. **Talk, then hand over.** A 3-year-old attends to one thing at a time and stops what they're doing to listen [W4][W2]. So the adult talks, then stops, then the child acts. Keep each run of talk to about 8 s (about 20 words at Sensei's recorded pace) before the child does something, even if that something is only a "ready" tap or saying a sound aloud.
7. **Wait 5–6 seconds after a question.** Teachers typically wait under a second [W6]. Sounds~Write asks for "four or five seconds" [L2] and the Reading Framework "at least six seconds" [W1 p134]. Then rephrase, don't repeat [W1 p134].
8. **Praise the work, specifically, and not every time.** "You did a good job drawing" beats "You are a good drawer" for 4-year-olds after a later mistake [W7]; inflated praise backfires for less confident children [W8]. The Reading Framework: praise "being specific about what they have done well" [W1 p51].
9. **Correct gently and end on the answer.** No negative comments [L1][L6]; model the right answer rather than making the child repeat a correction [W1 p136]; hand the task straight back [L6].
10. **Frame in full the first time, then fade.** First meeting: the whole frame and a demo. Next time: "Remember this game? I say a word, and you find it." After that: the name and go. Children join in more as a routine becomes familiar [W12], and the Reading Framework warns against routines that change so often that "too much time is lost in explanations" [W1 p56].

## 1. The anatomy of a great activity introduction

This is the order excellent Nursery and Reception teachers follow, whether the activity is a Sounds~Write word build, an oral blending game or "I spy". It maps closely onto Jonas's own model ("So first, I'm going to show you how to do it. Are you ready? And this is how this game goes. I will show you this, and you will do that. Do you want to give it a go now?"). Each step says what the teacher does, why, and how long it takes.

### Step 1. Get attention, warmly (1–3 s)

- **What:** the child's name (in the game: "ninja", or the child's name if a grown-up typed one), a friendly face turned to the child, and a consistent signal. Never a shout.
- **Why:** at 3–4 a child "can still only focus on one activity at a time" and "will stop when adult speaks, give their full attention to the adult, and then will continue" [W4]. Development Matters says for 3- and 4-year-olds: "say the child's name and then: 'Please stop and listen'", and its checkpoint for about age 3 asks whether the child can shift task "if you fully obtain their attention, for example, by using their name" [W2]. The Reading Framework recommends "Deciding on a signal to alert children to listen" [W1 p25].
- **In Super Ninja:** Sensei's portrait turning and glowing, a soft chime or the gong, and "Hello, ninja." That's the signal. It should be the same every time, so the child learns it.

### Step 2. Connect to what the child already knows (0–3 s, optional)

- **What:** "Do you remember the sun and the sock?" or "You heard /s/ at the start of sun. Today we'll find more words like that."
- **Why:** Sounds~Write: "Always begin your lesson by reviewing what you covered previously" [L2]. The Reading Framework lists "deliberately connecting current and past events ('Do you remember when…?')" among the moves of good adult talk [W1 p22] and "support the children to connect the new knowledge with their previous learning" [W1 p51]. On Day 2 of Reception, the Sounds~Write teacher starts by asking which word the class built yesterday [L5].
- **Skip it** on the very first game.

### Step 3. Name the game and say what it's for (3–5 s)

- **What:** "This game is called Ninja Ears. It's a listening game." Then the goal in the child's terms: "We're going to find the pictures I say." or "Today I'm going to teach you a new sound, and show you how we write it."
- **Why:** Sounds~Write: tell pupils "today, you are going to be showing them how to read and spell words with the /n/ sound" [L2]. The Reading Framework's model objective is "By the end of this week you will all be able to read these sounds; today we are learning the first one." [W1 p50]. A name makes the game findable again ("Remember Ninja Ears?"), which the later short frames depend on.
- **Explain any symbol** the moment it appears: "When you see the ear, it's time to listen." Young children take words and images literally. Fred Rogers had "I'm going to blow this up" redubbed as "I'm going to puff this up with some air" because children might expect an explosion [W14]. An ear that appears with no explanation, or "Ninja ears on!", is a metaphor the child has to decode.

### Step 4. Say how the game goes: who does what, in order (4–6 s)

- **What:** "Here's how it goes. I'll say a word. Then you tap its picture." Two steps, in the order they happen, with the actor named in each ("I'll…", "you…").
- **Why:** 3- and 4-year-olds are learning to "understand a question or instruction that has 2 parts" [W2]. The Reading Framework's advice for simplifying: say "'First we'll wash our hands. Then we'll go to lunch.' The sequence of events is clearer." [W1 p134]. Sounds~Write's oral blending game frames the roles the same way: "I am going to say the sounds in one of them. See if you can guess which one I'm saying" [L4]. Jonas: "I will show you this, and you will do that."
- **Anticipate the one likely mistake**, briefly: "Some pictures start with a different sound, so listen carefully." Sounds~Write notes that effective teachers "anticipate student errors and … alert them" [L2].

### Step 5. "Watch me first": the demonstration, thought aloud (5–8 s)

- **What:** "I'll go first. Watch me." Then do *one* item, narrating in the first person, pointing at each thing as it is named, and saying the reason for the choice: "My word is sun. Where's the sun? Here it is. I'll tap the sun." For a sound game: "I'm listening for the very first sound. Sssun. /s/. Sun starts with /s/."
- **Why:** Sounds~Write's general points quote Rosenshine: provide "'think alouds' as you model the steps in an activity" [L2]; Rosenshine: "The teacher modeling and thinking aloud while demonstrating how to solve a problem are examples of effective cognitive support" [W3]. The Sounds~Write model for a child who can't blend is a complete think-aloud: the teacher says the sounds, says "I can hear the word 'man'", asks "Did you hear 'man'?", and ends "Now you have a go" [L1]. The Reading Framework's list of good adult talk starts with "thinking out loud, modelling new language for children" and includes "explaining why things happen" [W1 p22].
- **Keep the child as watcher.** The demo speaks as "I", never as a command to the child ("Tap the sun!") while the paw does it. A question in the demo is marked as Sensei asking herself: "Hmm, which one starts with /m/?"
- **One demo item** is enough for a familiar kind of task; a brand-new mechanic may need a second.

### Step 6. Check readiness, and wait for the answer (2 s plus the child's tap)

- **What:** "Are you ready to have a go? Tap my paw!" or "Do you want to have a go now, or shall I show you again?" with two big buttons (go / show me again). Then the game waits.
- **Why:** it hands control to the child and makes sure they are looking before the key instruction. The Reading Framework's strategies include "Focus attention: make sure the child is looking towards you and listening to your request" and "Delay: make sure the child has waited until you have finished your request" [W1 p134]. Preschool TV does this as a ritual: *Play School*'s "Ready to knock? Turn the lock" [W13], and *Blue's Clues* hosts looking into the camera and asking "Will you help?" [W11]. Waiting works when the adult actually stops: Hanen's advice is to "Stop talking", lean in, and wait "for up to 10 seconds" [W5].
- **Never ask it rhetorically.** A question followed straight away by more talk teaches the child that questions don't need answers. If there is no button to answer with, don't ask; say "Here we go."
- **In Super Ninja** the readiness tap is the child's first action, which is what the age limits count (§3).

### Step 7. "Let's do it together" (first meeting only, one item, 5–8 s)

- **What:** "Let's do this one together." The child and Sensei do it side by side: "Say it with me: /s/." or "I'll start, and you finish. Sock starts with…" and the petal pulses for the child to tap.
- **Why:** "I do, we do, you do" is the Sounds~Write model: John Walker describes children learning to write sentences "because the process has been carefully modelled for them – I do, we do, you do" [L7]; an official case study credits "the 'I do, we do, you do' teaching model" [L8]; the official fluency blog describes moving "from guided support ('I do, we do') toward … ('you do')" [L9]. Sounds~Write's scripts are full of "we" moves: "Let's say the sounds again together and then the word" [L4]; "Now let's say the sounds and read the word" [L1]. The Reading Framework's "Sentence completion" strategy ("It is …") is a gentle together move [W1 p135].
- **Drop it** once the child has done this kind of item right before (§5 of SCRIPT_STYLE already fades it).

### Step 8. "Your turn": a clear call to action, then quiet (the turn)

- **What:** "Now it's your turn. Find the sock." Then silence while the child thinks. If there's no tap after about 5–6 s: rephrase ("Which picture is the sock?"), then offer help (a glow, then the paw pointing), never a nag. After the tap: the answer modelled back ("Sock! Sock starts with /s/.") and, every so often, specific praise.
- **Why:** the wait time evidence is in §3. Sounds~Write's praise in its own one-to-one demos is short and about the sound: "Good, you said that sound really well." [L1]. Correction is in the phrase bank (§2.10).

### Step 9. Wrap up and link to what's next (3–5 s)

- **What:** say what the child did and learned, then signal the transition: "You found the first sound in every word. Now let's put your pictures in your Sticker Book."
- **Why:** the Reading Framework ties listening to learning ("I could tell you were going to say the right answer, you were listening so carefully" [W2]) and asks for praise that is specific [W1 p51]. On CBeebies the presenters' job is exactly this link: "Presenters link the elements together to give the channel a relevance and sense of place for the audience" [W17]. A wrap-up also tells the child the game has ended, so the next screen isn't a surprise.

### Step 10. Next time: "Remember this one?"

- **Second meeting** (a later level, or a later session): "Remember Ninja Ears? I say a word, and you find its picture. Ready?" (about 5 s). No demo unless the child asks for Show me again or makes two errors.
- **Third meeting on:** the name and go. "Ninja Ears! Here's the first word."
- **After a long gap** (a week or more away, or a return after many other levels): the full frame again, shorter demo.
- **Hand more to the child each time.** *Blue's Clues* research found that as children mastered an episode they "became increasingly vocal and interactive" [W12], and that participation "greatly increased with repetition" [W11]. So a replay can invite the child to lead: "You know this one. You go first this time."

### 2.10 Gentle correction

| Line | When | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| "Hmm, let's listen again. Sssock." | first miss | Back to listening; no "No". |
| "That's the cat. Cat starts with a different sound. Listen: sssock." | a wrong picture | Names what they tapped, kindly, then models the target. |
| "Nearly! Listen to the very start: sssun." | close | "Nearly" only when it was close. |
| "Let's do that one together." | second miss | Turns the error into a we do. |
| "That's /m/. We need /s/." | second miss, sounds | SCRIPT_STYLE §10; ends on the target. |
| "It's this one. Sock starts with /s/. Now you tap it." | after help | Hand the task back [L6]. |
| "If this were 'bat', this would be a… /b/. Is this /b/?" | spelling a wrong letter | Sounds~Write [L1][L6]. |
| "This is /sh/. It's two letters, but it's one sound. Say /sh/ here." | reading a two-letter spelling as two sounds | Sounds~Write [L1]. |
| "Good try. Let's listen once more." | after any miss | Praises the attempt, not the error. |

Never: "No!", "Wrong!", a buzzer, a red cross, or asking the child to repeat the correction word for word. Sounds~Write's correction "avoids the use of negative comments" [L1]; the Reading Framework: "Model a correct response rather than asking them to repeat the correction" [W1 p136]; Development Matters: "Instead of correcting them, recast what the child said" [W2].

### 2.11 Specific praise

| Line | Notes |
|---|---|
| "You listened right to the very start of the word." | Praises the strategy. |
| "You heard /s/ at the start of sock." | The model is the praise (SCRIPT_STYLE §8). |
| "You said that sound really well." | Sounds~Write's own praise [L1]. |
| "You tapped them in order, this way. That's how ninjas read." | Names the behaviour. |
| "That was a tricky one, and you kept listening." | Effort and persistence [W10]. |
| "You found all three!" | Countable, true. |
| "You built the whole word by yourself." | Marks a you do success. |

Keep generic words ("Brilliant!", "Ace!") short and occasional, and never stack two praise lines [P2 §8]. Avoid trait praise ("You're so clever", "You're a ninja master!") except as a rare, earned milestone: 4-year-olds who heard "You are a good drawer" were more upset by a later mistake than those who heard "You did a good job drawing" [W7], and inflated praise lowers challenge-seeking in less confident children [W8].

| "Time for a rest soon. One more game?" | | Offers a choice near the end of a session. |

### 2.13 "Remember this one?" (replays and returns)

| When | Line |
|---|---|
| Second meeting | "Remember Ninja Ears? I say a word, and you find its picture. Ready? Tap my paw!" |
| Third meeting on | "Ninja Ears! Here's the first word." |
| Child leads | "You know how this one goes. You go first this time." |
| Recall question (with a tap answer) | "Can you remember what we do? Tap the picture of the sound we found last time." |
| Long gap | "It's been a while! Let me show you again. Watch me first." |
| A known sound in a new game | "You know this sound! It's /s/, like in sun." |

**Sounds~Write phrases that stay word for word** (teacher-language.md §10.7 [L1]): "Say the sounds and read the word." · "Say the sounds, and listen for the word." · "It's two letters, but it's one sound." · "This is /X/. Say /X/ here." · "This can be /a/, but in this word, it's /ae/." · "This is the way we spell /k/ in cat." (Early Years: "This is how we write /s/.") · "Same sound, different spellings." · "If you say the sounds, you can hear the word." (said explicitly, as Sounds~Write's Reception guidance asks [L5]). Framing talk wraps around these; it never paraphrases them.

## 3. Timing rules

The numbers below are for one child with a screen. Where the game's own limits already exist (docs/ARCHITECTURE.md §6.4 [P4]), they are shown next to the evidence.

| Rule | Number | Evidence | Fits the game's limits? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Run of talk before the child does something** | ≤ 8 s at 3–4 (about 20 words, 2–3 short sentences) | Single-channelled attention at 3–4: the child stops to listen, then acts [W4]; two-part instructions at most [W2]; Round 13 "too long and boring" [P1] | Matches "longest speech run 8 s" at ages 3 and 4 [P4] |
| **Talk before the first action in a game** | ≤ 10–12 s at 3, ≤ 15 s at 4, *if* a readiness tap counts as an action | [P4]; illustration A reaches the ear tap at about 10 s | Matches, provided "Are your ears ready? Tap the ear!" counts as the first action. Recommend it does; otherwise no game can ever be framed. |
| **Total first-meeting frame** (steps 1–6) | 15–25 s, split into ≤ 8 s runs with at least one tap | Teacher practice (§1); Jonas's model | New; the lesson time budgets (FIRST_MINUTES) will need to absorb it by cutting repeats, not the frame |
| **Short frame on later meetings** | ≤ 5 s | Fading [P2 §5]; repetition raises participation [W11][W12] | |
| **Sentence length** | about 4–10 words; one idea; at most two steps per instruction | Development Matters: "an instruction that has 2 parts" at 3–4 [W2]; at 4, children use sentences of 4 to 6 words themselves [W2] | SCRIPT_STYLE says 4–12 words [P2 §2]; tighten to 10 for ages 3–4 |
| **Wait after a question, before any re-prompt** | 5–6 s | Teachers usually wait "less than one second" [W6]; Sounds~Write: "give four or five seconds, longer for children with special needs" [L2]; Reading Framework: "Wait for at least six seconds" [W1 p134]; Hanen: "up to 10 seconds" [W5] | The idle ladder's 8 s glow is fine; nothing should be *said* before 5 s |
| **Second prompt** | a rephrase, not the same words | "Rephrase … Don't do this too quickly since the child may still be processing the first request" [W1 p134] | Today's re-ask repeats the same clip |
| **After "Are you ready?"** | wait for the tap, however long; nudge at 8 s, speak again at 16 s | Hanen [W5]; NAVIGATION §3.2 idle ladder [P5] | Reuse the held-step ladder |
| **Pause between sentences** | 300–600 ms; a longer beat (≈ 1 s) before the key sound or word | Children process spoken language more slowly; *Blue's Clues* pauses "long enough to give the youngest time to think, short enough for the oldest not to get bored" [W11] | Today's gaps are 150–500 ms (`Dojo.tsx` Learn) |
| **No talk over the child's action** | echo their tap only (the picture's name, the sound) | Single-channelled attention [W4]; SCRIPT_STYLE §9 | Already a rule |
| **Items between frames** | a turn every 5–10 s once play starts | FIRST_MINUTES lesson timings [P3] | |
| **Wrap-up** | ≤ 5 s, one praise line, one link | [W1 p51][W17]; SCRIPT_STYLE §8 no stacking | |

**Pace.** Sensei's recorded lines run at about 2.7 words a second. That is a sensible pace for these ages; the problem Jonas heard is not speed but missing content and the shouted delivery (§4, rows 1 and 16).

**Sessions.** A 3-year-old's game should stay around 60–90 s (FIRST_MINUTES), one school's nursery phonics session is 10 minutes [L4], and the Reading Framework says Reception phonics sessions "might be only ten minutes long in the first few days" [W1 p50]. Framing is paid for by cutting repeated items and repeated lines (SCRIPT_STYLE §1.1), never by dropping the frame.

## 4. What NOT to do, and why

| # | Don't | Why | Instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Bark a bare "Listen!" or start a game with "Listen…"** | It's a command with no object: the child doesn't know what to listen for or why, and at a raised pitch it sounds like a telling-off. Development Matters models a listening signal that names its object ("Listen carefully now for how many animals are on the broom") [W2]; Sounds~Write never uses "Listen" alone ("Listen carefully to hear the sounds that make up the word 'sat'") [L1]. Jonas: "weird shouted, listen". SCRIPT_STYLE §5.1 currently keeps "Listen…" before every stretched word; that rule leans too far. | "Get your listening ears ready." "Listen for the first sound." "Here it comes…" |
| 2 | **Show a symbol without saying what it means** (the ear, the tortoise and rabbit, a gem) | A 3-year-old reads images literally and can't guess a convention; preschoolers "tend to hear things literally" [W14]. Development Matters asks adults to explain unfamiliar words and concepts [W2]. | "When you see the ear, it's time to listen." Then let the symbol stand alone on later meetings. |
| 3 | **Use metaphors and slogans as instructions** ("Ninja ears on!", "Ninjas read this way!" with no demonstration) | Children take them literally; Rogers rewrote "blow this up" for the same reason [W14]. A slogan also carries no roles or steps. | Keep the flavour, add the meaning: "Let's put on our ninja ears. That means we listen really carefully." |
| 4 | **Skip the frame** (no name, no goal, no "I…, you…") | The child can't predict what happens or what counts as right. Sounds~Write: "State explicitly what the pupils are going to be learning" [L2]; Reading Framework: make sure children understand the objective [W1 p50]. FIRST_MINUTES §3 rule 3 ("No mode announcements") should be retired in favour of this research. | Steps 3–4 of §1. |
| 5 | **Give the child a command during Sensei's own demo** ("Let me show you! Tap the sun!" while the paw taps) | The child can't tell whose turn it is. At 3–4 they can't watch and act at once [W4]. | "I'll tap the sun." First person, then "Now it's your turn." |
| 6 | **Ask the child a question in the demo, then answer it yourself** ("Which one starts with /m/?" … "Man starts with /m/") | Teaches that questions don't need answers; confuses roles. | Mark it as thinking aloud: "Hmm, which one starts with /m/? Let me listen…" |
| 7 | **Ask "Are you ready?" and carry on talking** | A rhetorical question is noise to a child who is trying to answer it. Children's TV that works waits [W11][W13]; Hanen: "Stop talking" [W5]. | Ask only when there's a button to answer with, and wait for it. |
| 8 | **Talk too long, or explain things the child can't use yet** | Round 13's "too long and boring" [P1]. The Reading Framework warns against "too much time … lost in explanations" and a story about a sound "with so much detail that children focus on the story more than on what they need to learn" [W1 p56]. SCRIPT_STYLE rule 2 [P2]. | One frame, one demo, then turns. Story lines: one per game, after the frame. |
| 9 | **Speak in telegraphic fragments and spliced chains** ("A. Two letters, one sound. A. Two letters, one sound.") | Fragments sound robotic and carry no reasons; whole sentences with a subject and verb are what the child is learning to understand [W2][W1 p22]. Jonas's earlier transcript complaint [P2]. | Whole recorded sentences; splice only a sound or one word (SCRIPT_STYLE §7). |
| 10 | **Repeat the identical line when the child hasn't answered** | Repetition without change doesn't help a child who didn't understand; the Reading Framework says rephrase, and not too soon [W1 p134]. | Wait 5–6 s, then rephrase, then offer help. |
| 11 | **Answer or re-prompt within a second or two** | Typical teacher wait time is under a second, and longer waits improve answers [W6]; Sounds~Write asks for four or five seconds [L2]. | Silence for 5–6 s; a visual glow at 8 s. |
| 12 | **Stack generic or inflated praise** ("Amazing!" → "You're a ninja master!" → "You did it!") | Trait praise makes later mistakes feel like failure [W7][W9]; inflated praise lowers challenge-seeking in less confident children [W8]; stacking devalues it (SCRIPT_STYLE §8). | One short, specific line, at most every second right answer. |
| 13 | **Say "No", "Wrong", play a buzzer, or make the child repeat a correction** | Sounds~Write correction "avoids the use of negative comments" and is "a conversation about the way the English language works" [L1][L6]; model rather than make them repeat [W1 p136]. | §2.10: listen again, model, hand back. |
| 14 | **Give the whole answer at once, or segment a word the child is spelling** | Sounds~Write gives "only the missing piece" and hands the task back [L6]; "you do not segment the word for the student at all" in Sound Swap [L1]. | Stretch the word; point at the slot; let the child find it. |
| 15 | **Switch games or modes without a signal** | The child loses track; CBeebies presenters exist to "link the elements together" [W17]; the Reading Framework prefers stable routines [W1 p56]. | A wrap-up line and a "Next, we're going to…" link. |
| 16 | **Shout the instructions** (every line ending in "!") | 177 of 440 lines in `src/content/lines.ts` end in "!", including plain instructions ("Tap the sun!", "Your turn!", "Watch me first!"), and the TTS reads them as calls across a playground. Jonas heard it as shouting. Excitement belongs to celebrations; instructions are calm and warm. | Full stops for frames and instructions, "?" for real questions, "!" for celebrations and surprises. Re-record the instruction lines. |
| 17 | **Talk to a class** ("Everyone…", "boys and girls") | The game is one-to-one; *Play School*'s presenters "spoke directly to their audience" [W13], and contingent, personal address is what makes an on-screen adult someone a young child learns from [W16][W15]. | "Say it with me." "You…" |
| 18 | **Phrase things negatively or as threats** ("Don't tap yet!", "Baron will win!") | Rogers' writers rephrased every idea "in a positive manner" for preschoolers [W14]; a 3-year-old hears the forbidden action louder than the "don't". | "Wait for my paw… now it's your turn." |
| 19 | **Fade on the first meeting** | Fading is for familiar routines; a first meeting needs the whole frame (SCRIPT_STYLE §4 dosage applies to game frames too). | Full → short → name only, keyed to the child's ledger. |
| 20 | **Break Sounds~Write's rules inside friendly talk** (letter names, letters that "say" or "make" sounds, "muh") | The child hears the same words at school [L1]. Framing is ordinary English, but the phonics words stay exact. | §2.13's fixed phrases. |

## 5. What this means for the house style

For the script designer and the fix plan; nothing here is decided for them.

- **SCRIPT_STYLE.md stays right about repetition, dosage and praise.** Its §1 catalogue is the reason Lesson 1 felt long: the same lines said again, not the frame. Framing and de-duplication go together: every second a frame costs should come back from a cut repeat.
- **Three places lean too far towards brevity** and should change: FIRST_MINUTES §3 rule 3 (no mode announcements), SCRIPT_STYLE §5.1 ("Listen…" always, with no object), and the idea in SCRIPT_STYLE §5 of dropping the stem to a bare stimulus for 3-year-olds (fine at 5+, too bare at 3).
- **Add a readiness answer to the UI.** The anatomy needs a tap that means "yes, I'm ready": Sensei's paw as a high five, the ear badge, or the gong. The age limits in ARCHITECTURE §6.4 should count it as the first action.
- **Every game type needs a name** that Sensei says, so that "Remember Ninja Ears?" can exist.
- **The frame belongs to the ledger** like any explanation: full at the first meeting, short at the second, name-only after, full again after a long gap or two errors.
- **Hear it again and Show me again** (NAVIGATION §3.5–3.6) replay the frame and the demo, so nothing Sensei says in a frame is missable.
- **Punctuation audit before re-recording:** instructions end in full stops, questions in question marks, celebrations in exclamation marks.
